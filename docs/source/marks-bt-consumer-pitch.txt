BT Consumer: Proposal Narrative
Purpose of this document: a single, connected story we can walk a BT Consumer CEO through, explaining why the experience layer is now the competitive battleground for a mature-market telco, what that means in practice for BT’s customers, the architecture required to deliver it, and why Accenture is the right partner to build it. It is deliberately a narrative, not a solution design - the detail behind each section will firm up as we validate with BT stakeholders.
1. The customer is changing
The thesis: for a mature-market telco like BT, the connection itself has stopped being the differentiator. The differentiator is now the quality of the relationship built around it - and customer expectations of what “good” looks like have reset, driven by everyday exposure to AI. We are not predicting exactly how fast this moves. We are pointing at a direction of travel that is already visible in the data, and arguing that BT should be shaping it rather than reacting to it.
The economics say the old differentiators are exhausted
Network quality and price used to be enough to win and hold a customer. In UK mobile, that game has been played to a standstill: average revenue per customer fell from roughly £19 to £10 a month between 2015 and 2025, while EBITDA margins declined by around 18% in real terms over the same five years (Accenture, The Customer of the Intelligence Age, May 2026, citing the DSIT Mobile Market Review and Ofcom, 2025). Coverage and speed have become the price of entry, not a source of advantage - every serious competitor can match them. Meanwhile switching has never been easier: 1.6 million UK customers moved broadband or phone provider through One Touch Switch in the year to September 2025, and price rises of up to 13.4% in April 2026 are expected to push that higher still (Accenture, The Customer of the Intelligence Age, May 2026). If network and price no longer buy loyalty, something else has to.
Customers have a new reference point for “good,” and it isn’t other telcos
The shift isn’t abstract. More than half of customers say they would switch provider after a single bad service experience (Accenture, The Customer of the Intelligence Age, May 2026, citing Vodafone customer experience research, 2024). People are comparing BT not to EE or Vodafone, but to the best digital experience they had this week - often one shaped by generative AI, which has quietly reset what “the company already knows what I need” feels like. Accenture’s own research on AI and personal shopping found that 75% of consumers are now open to AI acting on their behalf, and 30% already trust an AI’s suggestion more than a human retailer, friend, or search engine (Accenture, Me, My Brand and AI, June 2025, as cited in The Customer of the Intelligence Age). That is the bar customers are unconsciously carrying into every interaction with BT - not “is this better than my last broadband provider,” but “is this as easy as everything else in my life now is.” 
But the same shift has created a trust deficit - and that cuts both ways
This is not simply a story of customers wanting more automation. Accenture Song’s Life Trends 2027 research finds a genuine split opening up: some customers want every last piece of friction engineered out of routine tasks, while a growing number are actively pushing back against frictionless design that leaves them feeling passed over or unheard - what the research calls “friction-maxxing” (Accenture Song, Life Trends 2027 - Executive Overview). The practical implication for BT is not “automate everything” or “keep everything human” - it’s knowing which moments are routine (where removing friction is a pure win) and which are load-bearing (where the customer needs to feel a human is genuinely present, accountable, and paying attention). Get that split wrong in either direction and it shows up as churn.
Trust itself has also become a harder currency to earn and easier to lose. 76% of consumers say they’d switch to a brand that handles their data and AI use transparently, even at a higher price, and 57% say they would stop using a company altogether if it can’t explain how its AI uses their data (Accenture, Trust is the Product, May 2026, citing the Relyance AI Consumer Trust Survey, December 2025). At the same time, there’s a widening gap between what customers want and what they believe brands can deliver responsibly: 69% want a personalised experience, but only 38% trust a brand to deliver one without crossing a line - the “personalisation gap” (Accenture, Trust is the Product, May 2026, citing NIQ, From Aisle to Algorithms, 2026). Regulation is starting to codify this: the EU AI Act’s Article 50, which took effect on 2 August 2026, now requires disclosure and explainability wherever AI shapes a customer interaction (Accenture, Trust is the Product, May 2026). For a company handling network, billing, and household data at BT’s scale, that combination - high expectation, low trust, tightening regulation - is exactly where the experience has to be engineered deliberately, not assembled from whatever each channel happens to do today.
Why this matters specifically for BT
BT operates in one of the more exposed corners of this shift: a mature, saturated, price-competitive market, with new entrants explicitly designing around the absence of legacy technical debt. Revolut’s move into UK and German mobile plans in April 2025, positioned as an AI-native MVNO with no legacy BSS/OSS estate to work around, is a live example of a challenger that can build the experience layer first and bolt the network on afterwards (Accenture, The Customer of the Intelligence Age, May 2026). BT doesn’t have - and doesn’t need - that luxury of starting from a blank sheet. But it does need to decide, deliberately, what the experience layer looks like on top of the network it already has, rather than let it emerge channel by channel.
The prize for getting this right is real and quantifiable in outline, even if the specific number for BT still needs testing: Accenture’s analysis across telco clients points to a 5–10 percentage point EBITDA margin opportunity, a 30–50% reduction in cost-to-serve for routine contact, and a 3–5 percentage point churn reduction from intercepting customers after a single bad experience before it becomes a decision to leave (Accenture analysis, drawing on the TM Forum Autonomous CX Index and Vodafone customer experience research, 2024, as cited in The Customer of the Intelligence Age). These are illustrative ranges from cross-industry analysis, not a BT-specific forecast - we’d want to size this properly against BT’s own numbers before it goes anywhere near a board pack. 
The claim we’re making is narrow and testable: in a market where network and price have stopped differentiating, and where customers’ reference point for “good service” is now set by AI-native experiences elsewhere in their lives, the experience layer - proactive, personal, and honestly explained - is where BT’s next real advantage has to be built.
2. What that means for BT
If the experience layer is the battleground, the next question is concrete: what does BT actually do differently? Not in the abstract - in the moments that make up a customer’s actual relationship with BT. Below are illustrative examples of the kind of moment we mean. They are not proposals for BT’s roadmap and they are not drawn from BT’s own data - they’re intended to make the argument tangible, and we’d expect to replace or refine every one of them once we’ve validated what’s real inside BT today.
Moment 1 - The first seven days
Most churn risk in home broadband is set in motion in the first week, not the twelfth month. A router that’s never been correctly set up, a customer who never completes activation, a family that quietly reverts to their old provider’s leftover kit because nobody noticed the new line wasn’t actually being used - these are silent failures. They don’t generate a complaint; they generate a customer who was never really onboarded, and who churns three months later for a reason that traces straight back to day one. The response BT could build isn’t a survey or a welcome call script - it’s a system that notices non-activation as it happens and treats it as a trigger, not a data point to review at month-end.
Moment 2 - The silent router isn’t always a problem
BT already has a constant, low-level diagnostic signal from every connected router: is it online, is it being used, has traffic dropped to zero. Today that signal probably gets treated the same way for every customer. But the same “router went quiet at 9pm” event means something completely different depending on who it’s happening to. For one household, it’s normal - they switch everything off overnight, every night, and have done for years. For another, it’s the middle of a Champions League night, the household has three teenagers streaming, and a dead connection is a crisis that will generate a complaint within the hour if BT doesn’t get there first. The diagnostic signal is identical. The right response is not. What tells BT the difference isn’t better network monitoring - it’s knowing the household well enough to interpret the same signal correctly.
Moment 3 - Proactive versus reactive is a memory problem, not a channel problem
The instinct is to frame “proactive engagement” as a channel or automation question - more SMS nudges, more app notifications. It isn’t. A message sent at the wrong time, referencing the wrong context, or repeating something the customer already told BT last month does more damage to trust than saying nothing at all. Genuinely proactive engagement - reaching a customer before they have to call, with something that’s actually useful to them specifically - depends entirely on BT knowing enough about that customer’s situation, preferences, and history to get the timing and the content right. Get the memory wrong and “proactive” just becomes “more noise.”
Moment 4 - One household, several products, one relationship
A family with BT mobile, BT broadband, and BT TV - or a mix of BT, EE, and Plusnet products, if that’s genuinely how the household resolves across brands - should not have to re-explain their situation every time they touch a different part of BT. Right now, we don’t know how connected that view already is inside BT (this is one of the open questions in our evidence log), but the customer experience of a fragmented view is a familiar failure mode across telcos: three separate relationships that all happen to send bills to the same address, rather than one household BT actually understands.
The common thread
Every one of these moments is a variation on the same underlying question: given a signal, what does BT know about this customer that changes the right response? Diagnostics, activation triggers, and contact-preference data all already exist somewhere in BT’s estate in some form. What turns them into a good customer experience - rather than a generic, one-size-fits-all response, or worse, the wrong response confidently delivered - is what BT remembers about the person on the other end. That’s the thread that connects Section 1’s argument to Section 3’s architecture: differentiation isn’t a new channel or a smarter algorithm, it’s customer memory, applied consistently, at the moment it’s needed.
A method note: we’ve built this kind of moment-by-moment thinking out properly before, on a comparable telco engagement, mapping several dozen customer moments end-to-end from signal to action to feedback loop. We’re not importing that client’s answer here - the market, the brands, and the starting architecture are all different - but the method of starting from real customer moments and working backwards to the data and decisioning they require is one we’d bring to BT directly.
3. The architecture this needs
None of Section 2’s moments are deliverable with a smarter chatbot or a better dashboard. They need five things working together, in this order, as a connected system rather than five separate initiatives.
1. Customer memory - the moat
Most telcos already have a customer 360 view of a kind: transactions, products held, billing history, contact records. That’s necessary but not sufficient, and it isn’t what makes Moment 2 (the silent router) or Moment 3 (proactive done right) possible. What’s missing is the softer layer: how this household prefers to be contacted, and when; what “normal” looks like for their usage; what’s already been said to them and by which channel; who else is in the household and what matters to them. Accenture’s Trust is the Product research frames this as six layers of memory - identity, behavioural, service, context, emotional, and intentional - and argues that a memory which only updates in a nightly batch job decays into a liability rather than an asset (Accenture, Trust is the Product, May 2026). The test we’d apply to any BT data product is simple: can it be used in the moment of a live interaction or decision, not just pulled up afterwards in a report? This is what turns a customer record into customer memory - and because it accumulates and compounds over time, in a way that’s specific to BT’s own relationship history, it’s genuinely hard for a competitor to replicate. That’s what makes it a moat rather than a feature.
2. The reasoning and planning engine
Memory on its own doesn’t do anything. The second component takes a live signal - the router event, the missed activation, the usage pattern - combines it with what memory says about that specific customer, and decides what, if anything, should happen: do nothing (because this is normal for this household), offer support, make a sale, trigger an engineering visit, or run a backend optimisation with no customer-visible action at all. Critically, this isn’t a black-box recommendation - the decision and the reasoning behind it get logged, so it can be checked, explained, and improved. This is a more capable version of what BT’s evidence base suggests already exists in some form today around next-best-action for commercial and pricing decisions - the extension is widening its reach into service and network moments, and making every decision explainable and auditable, not just executed.
3. Turning the decision into a safe, governed action
A decision that stays inside a dashboard has changed nothing for the customer. The plan has to connect to real tooling - sending the SMS, placing the call, booking the engineer, running the diagnostic, adjusting the backend configuration - through channels that are genuinely available to act on, with clear rules about which actions a system can take on its own and which need a person to sign off. This is where most agentic CX ambitions actually stall: it’s substantially easier to build a good recommendation than it is to build the safe, auditable path from that recommendation to a real-world action across care, digital, network, and field operations. We’d treat this “decision-to-action” layer as equally important to the memory and reasoning components, not as an implementation afterthought.
4. The data flywheel - closing the loop
The fourth component is what makes this a compounding capability instead of a one-off project: did the plan work? Was the fault actually resolved, was the customer satisfied, did the offer convert? That outcome gets written back into memory and used to reinforce what worked or trigger a rethink of what didn’t - without a person needing to review each case individually, operating within clearly defined constraints and escalation rules. This is the same idea BT stakeholders have themselves described as wanting a “self-learning architecture” - our contribution is turning that ambition into a specific, testable mechanism: does this loop close today, even for a single use case, and if not, what’s the smallest version we could get closing within weeks rather than quarters.
5. The governance layer - what makes the other four deployable
Running beneath all four components is an explicit policy layer, and it belongs here as the fifth element in its own right, not as a caveat attached to the other four. It defines what the system may do unsupervised, what requires a named human to sign off, which data may be used for which purpose, and what “correct” looks like for each class of decision - and it records every decision, the reasoning behind it, and the outcome, so explainability becomes an artefact BT can show a regulator or a customer rather than a principle it asserts. With Article 50 of the EU AI Act in force since August 2026, and 57% of consumers saying they would leave a company that cannot explain how its AI uses their data (Accenture, Trust is the Product, May 2026), this is not the brake on the experience layer but the licence to operate it - and the reason BT can move faster than a competitor relitigating risk with every new use case. It should be stood up alongside the first use case, not retrofitted onto a live decisioning estate.
Why this has to be one system, not five projects
The reason to present this as a single architecture rather than five separate initiatives is that each component is close to useless without the others. Memory without a reasoning engine is just a better report. A reasoning engine without governed action is a recommendation nobody acts on. Action without a feedback loop never gets better. And none of it is safe without governance running through all of it. This is also exactly where most “data transformation” programmes go wrong - they build the memory layer beautifully and stop, because nobody designed the decision, action, and feedback layers alongside it from day one.
4. Why Accenture
There’s a version of this proposal that BT could reasonably be sceptical of: another consultancy showing up promising to fix the data. We’d want to open by acknowledging that scepticism directly, because it’s the right instinct.
The last thing BT needs is a data programme. Fixing data quality and structure without a clear, tested view of exactly how that data will be used to change a real customer moment is a way to spend a great deal of money and end up no closer to the outcome. We’ve seen this pattern before: a beautifully built customer data platform that nobody connects to a decision, or a decisioning capability with no safe way to act on what it recommends. The five-part architecture in Section 3 is deliberately designed to avoid that trap - every data product exists because a specific customer moment needs it, not because a data strategy says it should exist. 
What makes this a job for one partner rather than a committee of specialists is that it genuinely needs all five things at once, and they don’t compose well if built by five different teams with five different mandates: 
Telco domain understanding. Knowing how BSS/OSS, network diagnostics, and multi-brand commercial models actually work, so the architecture is grounded in what’s real inside a telco, not a generic enterprise data pattern relabelled for BT. 
Consumer behaviour and engagement design. Designing the actual moment a customer experiences, informed by the kind of behavioural and trust research underpinning Section 1, rather than stopping at the backend decision. 
Agentic architecture at scale. The engineering discipline that makes the reasoning, decisioning, and governed-action layers in Section 3 safe, explainable, and auditable in live production, beyond a proof of concept. 
The underlying data and platform capability. Able to work with BT’s existing systems as they are, rather than requiring a rebuild before anything can be delivered. 
Accenture’s Customer of the Intelligence Age and Trust is the Product research point to specific accountability roles that tend to emerge once organisations take this seriously - a Chief Customer Officer accountable for the end-to-end experience, a Memory and Trust Officer accountable for how customer data is used and explained, an AgentOps lead accountable for how agents behave in production, and a human escalation lead accountable for the moments a system correctly hands back to a person (Accenture, The Customer of the Intelligence Age and Trust is the Product, May 2026). We’re not recommending BT adopt this org chart as written - that’s exactly the kind of thing this engagement needs to test against BT’s own operating model, not assume - but it’s a useful signal that this succeeds or fails on operating-model design as much as on technology, and we bring the same rigour to both. 
The pitch, in one line: BT doesn’t need a data programme, or an AI programme, or a CX programme run as three separate initiatives that meet in a steering committee. It needs one partner who can hold the customer moment, the data, the decision, and the safe action together as a single, accountable system - and who has done exactly this kind of work, end to end, in telco before.
